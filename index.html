01 B2B · Thought Leadership

One Company, Three Personal Brands.

Who are they

A CEO and his team members.

Challenge

Managing three LinkedIn accounts from the same company and industry comes with an obvious problem: How do you make three people talk about the same space without making them sound like the same person?

Approach

Instead of creating one content strategy and replicating it across three accounts, I built three individual voices under one larger brand narrative.

Strategy

CEO perspective:
More focused on leadership, business, people, experiences, case studies, and the broader ideas behind the industry.

Team perspective:
Content built around her own expertise, observations, and experiences as someone working within the company.

Result

Grew all three profiles by 11.5%, 17%, and 30.2% respectively, while keeping each personal brand distinct and authentic. More importantly, each profile developed its own content identity.

The strategy was built around three major narratives: 1. Why the aftermarket is broken Industry problems, inefficiencies, and outdated processes. 2. Excel is great… until it becomes your database.

This piece explores what happens when a familiar spreadsheet tool starts being used as a system of record.

The writing moves through data integrity, version control, formulas, structure and the risks of treating a flexible spreadsheet like a database.

The central communication challenge was to make a technical B2B problem immediately understandable through an experience many professionals already know.

The post closes by shifting the discussion back to the reader and asking how much time their organisation spends fixing spreadsheets instead of working with the information inside them.

Amazon spent $1.2 billion on Zoox. And now, they’re giving away free rides.

The post uses Zoox's free robotaxi rides in Las Vegas as the starting point for a broader conversation around autonomous mobility.

Walmart’s CFO just made history. But not for the reasons you might think.

The post takes a corporate and market development and turns it into a human-readable business story.

It introduces the executive, creates curiosity around the headline, then moves through Walmart's performance, margins, comparable sales, e-commerce growth and the broader strategic picture.

The objective is not simply to list financial numbers, but to explain why the numbers matter and what they reveal about the company's direction.

It ends with a question that invites the audience to think about Walmart's position as a major global retailer.
